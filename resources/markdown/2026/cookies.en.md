# 2. What are cookies?
A cookie is a small file of letters and numbers stored on the computer, mobile device or other equipment from which a user accesses the internet. It is placed there at the request of a web server to a browser (for example Chrome or Safari) and is entirely passive: it contains no software, viruses or spyware, and cannot read anything else on the device.

# 3. What are cookies used for?
They let a site recognise a device and present content in a way that suits the person using it. Cookies make browsing more comfortable and support our work in offering a usable site — remembering privacy preferences, for instance. They are also used to prepare aggregate, anonymous statistics that help us understand how people use our pages, so we can improve their structure and content, without identifying anyone personally.

# 4. What kinds of cookies are there?
Two: session and persistent. Session cookies are temporary and remain on the device only until the session ends or the browser is closed. Persistent cookies stay for the period set in the cookie itself, or until the user deletes them.

# 6. Do cookies contain personal data?
Cookies do not themselves require personal information in order to work and, in most cases, do not personally identify internet users. Personal data collected through the use of cookies is collected only to make certain features work for the user, and is encrypted in a way that makes access by unauthorised parties impossible.

# 7. Deleting cookies
Applications used to browse the web generally allow cookies to be saved by default. These settings can be changed so that the browser blocks cookies automatically, or tells you each time cookies are being sent to your device. Detailed information about managing cookies can be found in your browser's settings. Restricting the use of cookies may affect how parts of a website work.

# 8. Why cookies matter to the internet
Cookies are central to the internet working smoothly, helping produce a browsing experience adapted to each person's preferences and interests. Refusing or disabling them can make some sites unusable. Refusing them does not mean you stop seeing online advertising — only that it can no longer take your browsing behaviour into account.

Examples of important uses of cookies that do not require a user account:

* Content and services adapted to a user's preferences
* Offers adapted to a user's interests, and remembered passwords
* Remembering child-protection filters for internet content (family mode, safe search)
* Limiting how often an advertisement is shown to a particular person
* Providing more relevant advertising
* Measurement, optimisation and analytics — confirming a certain level of traffic to a site, what content is viewed, and how someone arrived (through a search engine, directly, from another site)

Sites carry out this analysis of their own use in order to improve them for the people using them.

# 9. Security and privacy
Cookies are not viruses. They use plain text formats. They are not made up of pieces of code, so they cannot be executed or run by themselves. They cannot duplicate or replicate themselves onto other networks. Because they cannot do any of these things, they cannot be considered viruses.

Cookies can, however, be used for negative purposes. Because they store information about preferences and browsing history, both on one site and across several, they can be used as a form of spyware. Many anti-spyware products are aware of this and routinely flag cookies for removal during scans.

Browsers generally have privacy settings built in, offering different levels of cookie acceptance, validity periods and automatic deletion after a site has been visited.

# Other security matters relating to cookies
Because protecting one's identity is valuable and is every internet user's right, it is worth knowing what problems cookies can create. Since information travels constantly in both directions between browser and website, an attacker or unauthorised person intervening along that path could intercept what a cookie contains.

Though rare, this can happen if the browser connects to the server over an unencrypted network — an unsecured WiFi network, for example.

Other cookie-based attacks rely on incorrect cookie settings on servers. If a website does not require the browser to use encrypted channels only, attackers can exploit this to trick browsers into sending information over unsecured channels, and then use that information to gain unauthorised access. Choosing the right way to protect personal information matters.

# Advice for safe and responsible browsing
Because of their flexibility, and because most large and frequently visited sites use them, cookies are close to unavoidable. Disabling them would prevent access to the most widely used sites, among them YouTube, Gmail and Yahoo.

Install and keep anti-spyware applications up to date. Many applications that detect and prevent spyware also detect attacks on websites, stopping the browser from reaching sites that might exploit browser vulnerabilities or download harmful software.

# Keep your browser up to date
Many cookie-based attacks work by exploiting weaknesses in older browser versions. Cookies are everywhere and cannot be avoided if you want access to the best and largest sites on the internet, local or international. With a clear understanding of how they work and what they offer, you can take the security measures that let you browse with confidence.

Disabling cookies, or refusing to accept them, can make certain sites impractical or difficult to use. Refusing them also does not mean you will stop seeing online advertising.

Your browser can be set not to accept cookies at all, or to accept them only from a particular site. But if, for example, you are not registered using cookies, you will not be able to leave comments.

All modern browsers offer the ability to change cookie settings. These are usually found under "options" or in your browser's "preferences" menu.

To understand these settings, your browser's own help pages are the best place to look.
